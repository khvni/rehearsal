Earning Autonomy: From Missions to Workstreams

A team hands an agent a large goal, approves the plan, and returns later to completed work.

Luke AlvoeiroFactoryMember of Technical Staff

Day 1 livestream · 3:42:45–3:58:05 on YouTube. The player starts and stops at this session’s boundaries.

Introduction#

A team hands an agent a large goal, approves the plan, and returns later to completed work. That was the promise of Factory's Missions. Luke Alvoeiro says it worked best when the goal was clear and verifiable, but adoption exposed a problem: much software work does not stay fixed long enough to plan all of it in advance.

His talk follows the redesign from a long-running assignment to a standing workstream. The central change is how autonomy is earned, observed, and adjusted rather than handed over all at once.

Long-running work needs room to change direction#

Missions assumed substantial upfront planning. Once implementation started, the product gave little room for questions or ongoing negotiation. That fit some migrations and large projects, but not every kind of work.

More capable review created a second problem. Adversarial review at every milestone could discover enough additional work to expand a mission dramatically. Alvoeiro describes an example growing from roughly sixty features to more than two hundred, bringing its token bill and duration along with it. Finding more possible improvements is not the same as finishing the agreed job.

Finally, context remained tied to one person. Other members of the team could not easily reuse what the mission had learned. The redesign had to address scope, shared learning, and ongoing coordination together.

3:47:25 ↗

Make the worker efficient before multiplying it#

Alvoeiro compares an organization running many agents to an operating system: work needs scheduling, isolated execution, and shared state. He first revisits the worker itself.

At 03:47:25, he describes programmatic tool calling. Instead of returning to the model between every operation, the model writes a small program that invokes tools and prints only relevant results. His example moves 150 Linear tickets. The program keeps repetitive execution out of the model's conversational loop and avoids returning every intermediate detail as context.

That matters more on long horizons. One unnecessary inference request, oversized tool result, or cache miss can be repeated thousands of times. Efficiency at the inner loop determines whether keeping many workers active is practical.

Workers also need somewhere to run. A single machine becomes a point of contention when agents share files and system resources. Factory's environment definitions package repositories, dependencies, and tools into reusable sandboxes, while a control plane coordinates execution on the organization's chosen compute.

A workstream has a standing goal and an owner#

A rigid graph of agent steps did not solve the orchestration problem. Alvoeiro says it recreated the inflexibility and cost of Missions. The replacement is a workstream with a goal, an identity, an environment, and memory.

An orchestrator delegates areas of ownership, routes new tasks to the appropriate workers, and receives completion reports, blockers, or changes in the plan. Work can begin conversationally and later acquire recurring intake: for example, checking two Slack channels for bug reports and deduplicating them against existing issues and error reports.

The resulting unit is a proposed change rather than a pile of messages. A complaint in Slack and a matching error report can become one specification. The owner can review that specification before implementation starts.

Identity is part of the design. Alvoeiro describes starting under a person's access and later moving the workstream to an organization-controlled service account. A standing responsibility should not depend forever on one person's session or credentials.

3:52:03 ↗

Remove checkpoints one at a time#

At 03:52:03, Alvoeiro describes levels of autonomy. At the initial level, a person reviews the specification, the PR, and the decision to merge. Higher levels remove checkpoints as the work demonstrates that it can meet the expected standard.

This turns trust into a sequence of observable decisions. The team need not decide on day one whether an agent should own CI health without supervision. It can first check whether the agent identifies the right problems, then whether its proposed changes are sound, then whether its finished PRs deserve less manual intervention.

Shared files and versioned skills preserve what the workers learn. Findings from parallel investigations can be written to a common workspace and synthesized by the orchestrator. Related PRs and review feedback can become context for the next task instead of being rediscovered from scratch.

3:54:30 ↗

The change must not write its own approval policy#

Autonomous implementation still meets a separate validation layer. Alvoeiro describes code review, security review, and QA against a live preview, followed by a risk judge with the actual diff and a checkout it can build and test.

At 03:54:30, the slide specifies that the judge reads policy from the development branch, never from the PR, and is not told its own thresholds.

The separation matters because a proposed change should not be able to weaken the rule under which it is being assessed. Passing implementation and passing review are distinct jobs.

Feedback continues after merge. Alvoeiro describes mining acted-on review comments, tracing reverts and hotfixes to their originating PRs, and using production errors to improve future guidelines. The metric he emphasizes is the share of merges that never need a revert or follow-up fix. He calls it the regret-free merge rate, and compares agent-written and human-written changes rather than treating output volume as sufficient evidence of success.

Start with a signal the team already neglects#

Factory's reported internal results include hundreds of PRs over a weekend and workstreams handling recurring bug reports and flaky tests. Those figures describe its own operation, not a guaranteed result of enabling an autonomy level elsewhere.

The closing exercise is more useful than the volume: name the signals that already tell the team its software should change. A failing CI job, a recurring Slack complaint, an error spike, and an abandoned ticket are all candidates. Pick one that consumes too much attention, begin with human checkpoints, and establish validation before expanding authority.

The factory grows from a responsibility the team can name and measure. Autonomy advances when the evidence warrants the next checkpoint disappearing.
