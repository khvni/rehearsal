Green Pull Requests Can Still Break the Trunk

Two pull requests pass CI against the same base. Merge the first, then the second, and the combined branch breaks. Neither green result tested the state that ultimately reached the trunk.

Manjari AkellaUber

Introduction#

This edition uses the complete standalone recording, including the post-merge validation and recovery discussion missing from the livestream excerpt. Text links use the standalone clock; the retained livestream screenshot is labeled separately.

Two pull requests pass CI against the same base. Merge the first, then the second, and the combined branch breaks. Neither green result tested the state that ultimately reached the trunk.

Preetam Dwivedi and Manjari Akella begin with that ordinary integration problem, then scale it to Uber's monorepos. They describe six language-based repositories serving more than 5,000 developers, with tens of thousands of commits and pull requests each week. Their focus is the Go repository, the largest and the one presenting the most acute scaling challenges.

A merge queue protects the trunk by testing likely future combinations before landing changes. But that protection consumes build capacity. As AI increases the rate at which engineers produce changes, demand can rise faster than the system's ability to validate those futures. Uber's response eventually changes the guarantee: not every landed commit is known green, but consumers can use a separately maintained, validated reference.

0:02:57 ↗

Predict the future without serializing every change#

At 00:02:57, Dwivedi introduces SubmitQueue. For two changes, possible futures include A alone, B alone, and A followed by B. The queue performs speculative validation so the combination that lands has been tested, rather than assuming two independently green PRs must compose.

Three optimizations make that approach practical. Target analysis identifies changes affecting independent parts of the build graph, allowing them to be built and merged without waiting on one another. Probabilistic speculation uses historical build information and change characteristics to prioritize the speculative builds most likely to be needed. Smaller changes can bypass a large, slow-building request instead of inheriting its delay simply because it arrived first.

These mechanisms had worked at an earlier arrival rate. The speakers report roughly 90 requests per hour in January 2026 and 130 by March, attributing the approximately 44% increase primarily to AI-generated changes. The p95 land time went from around 30 minutes to three or four hours. Keeping the trunk green was still possible, but the waiting time had become unacceptable.

Akella presents three responses in sequence: admission gating, deferrals, and conflict relaxation. They address different constraints, and each introduces a different trade-off.

0:08:03 ↗

Admission control makes developers the schedulers#

At 00:08:03, the first intervention adds a gate before the active queue. A small change touches relatively few targets. An expensive change may span a large part of the graph and need validation against much of the other work in flight.

During peak hours, admitting small changes while rejecting expensive ones protects shared capacity. Akella reports that the business-hours land rate rose from approximately 130 to 160 per hour, an improvement of more than 20%.

The cost falls unevenly. An engineer maintaining a widely used library naturally produces broad changes. Rejecting those requests does not eliminate the work; it makes their authors return outside peak hours and try again. The platform improves its throughput by transferring scheduling effort to a subset of developers.

Deferrals repair that interaction. Instead of rejecting an expensive request, persist it in a secondary queue. When the admission policy and available capacity permit, release it into the active queue. The developer submits once, and the platform handles the later attempt.

That solves the need to wake early, stay late, or watch for a favorable window. It does not, by itself, reduce how long the expensive change waits to land. Akella explicitly separates better developer experience from faster completion.

0:14:30 ↗

Weak overlaps can create a large connected component#

The next bottleneck is structural. Suppose A overlaps B, B overlaps C, and the chain continues through F. Even if A and F have no direct interaction, the overlapping links group all six changes into one connected component. Target analysis has discovered a dependency chain that limits parallel work.

Conflict relaxation replaces the binary question “do these requests overlap?” with “how strong is their overlap?” Strong links remain; selected weaker links can be dropped to achieve a configured amount of parallelism.

At 00:14:30, Akella introduces Jaccard scoring. The slide defines Jaccard similarity as shared targets divided by distinct targets. The ranking combines ordinary target overlap with signals from newly introduced dependency edges. Importantly, the slide labels the result a ranking signal, not a failure probability. A low score does not certify that two changes cannot break each other.

Original livestream screenshot at 06:00:40, not the standalone clock. Dropping the B–C and D–E bridges turns one six-change component into three two-change queues. The corresponding explanation begins around 15:06 in the complete recording.

In the example, six requests and a configured 50% parallelism target call for at least three independently progressing queues. Dropping the two weakest bridges supplies those components. This is not free parallelism: it is a deliberate decision to stop pre-validating some interactions together.

Preserve speculative work when the queue changes#

There is another scheduling choice beneath the graph. The platform could recompute all relationships whenever a request enters or leaves. That would allow continual global adjustment, but it would also reshuffle the graph and reduce the usefulness of speculative work already scheduled.

Instead, the implementation assigns relationships when a new request arrives and preserves those decisions. Subsequent arrivals get their own assignment. This local, entry-time approach retains more previously planned speculative builds.

Throughput depends on more than the theoretical number of independent tasks. It also depends on how much completed or in-flight validation remains useful as the queue evolves.

Akella reports that relaxation raised throughput from about 160 lands per hour to nearly 250, sometimes higher during peak periods. It also improved high-percentile land times. The cost is explicit: some combinations now land without having been tested together. Main can break again.

0:20:03 ↗

One history, two moving references#

The recovered continuation starts from that consequence rather than hiding it. A and B pass their independent checks after their conflict is relaxed. A lands, then B lands, and their combination fails. The initial integration problem has returned, so the system needs a new obligation: validate landed states and maintain a usable known-good reference.

At 00:20:03, Dwivedi introduces Stovepipe, Uber's post-land validation system. SubmitQueue advances main as changes land. Stovepipe advances verified/main only after validation passes.

At 00:21:15, both references share one commit history, but they no longer promise the same thing.

Start with both references at a known-good commit A. Commits B, C, and D land, moving main to D. Stovepipe validates the newly landed range on top of A. If validation fails, verified/main stays at A.

More commits can subsequently arrive. Suppose main reaches G and validation of the accumulated state now passes. Stovepipe advances verified/main to G. The verified reference follows passing states, rather than pretending every intervening commit has independently become safe.

This is a shift in where confidence is represented. Landing records integration progress. Validation records evidence that a particular resulting state builds and passes the relevant checks. Separating those two facts lets the merge queue move faster without asking consumers to treat an unverified head as known good.

0:22:55 ↗

Recovery still requires an on-call engineer#

At 00:22:55, Dwivedi explains what happens when post-land validation detects a break. Stovepipe alerts the on-call engineer. The engineer prepares a fix or revert, obtains review, and lands it through SubmitQueue. Stovepipe then validates the new state and moves the verified reference when the checks pass.

The talk does not describe fully automated recovery as already deployed. Nor does it supply a measured breakage rate or recovery-time distribution. Those omissions matter when interpreting the throughput gains: the design deliberately accepts a new operational burden, and the presentation explains the workflow for handling it rather than proving that burden is negligible.

The results slide, labeled as of October 2026, reports roughly 300 peak lands per hour and a p95 land time of approximately 30 minutes. That is about a 233% increase from January's roughly 90-per-hour baseline. These are Uber's reported results for the evolved system, not a controlled estimate of the isolated effect of each optimization.

The hybrid design has three next steps#

The speakers describe SubmitQueue and Stovepipe as open-source projects, then outline further work. Batching related changes could reduce repeated validation and improve resource efficiency. Project-scoped validation could answer whether a particular service is green without treating every repository failure as globally blocking. Automating diagnosis, fixes, and reverts could reduce the manual recovery delay.

Those are future directions in the talk, distinct from the shipped whole-repository verified/main pointer and human-led recovery process.

The final design remains hybrid. Pre-merge checks still test the interactions judged important enough to retain. Relaxation permits selected interactions to be checked after landing, and post-land validation establishes the known-good state consumers can use. The change is not “stop testing because AI writes too much code.” It is a redistribution of validation, accompanied by an explicit distinction between landed and verified.

Admission gating protects capacity. Deferrals stop making developers manually resubmit. Conflict relaxation buys parallelism by weakening a pre-merge guarantee. Stovepipe supplies the corresponding post-merge validation boundary, and on-call recovery repairs the failures that boundary detects. The complete system makes the trade legible: faster integration requires a new place to hold confidence and a concrete plan for restoring it.
