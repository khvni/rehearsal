Beyond Generation: Fast, Structured AI Decisions with Jev at Glean

A search assistant makes many decisions that do not require it to write anything.

Chinmay GoyalGleanSoftware Engineer

Day 1 livestream · 5:34:33–5:50:08 on YouTube. The player starts and stops at this session’s boundaries.

Introduction#

A search assistant makes many decisions that do not require it to write anything. It must identify the kind of query, choose the right expert, select a follow-up suggestion, or judge whether a citation supports a claim. Chinmay Goyal starts with those bounded choices rather than with an attempt to replace the language model everywhere. At Glean, his question is where a promptable decision model belongs between a general-purpose LLM and a fine-tuned classifier.

5:34:33 ↗

The middle ground between prompts and training#

At 05:34:33, Goyal distinguishes generation from selection. If the possible outputs are already known, generating a string introduces work the application may not need: decoding, parsing, and checking that the answer is actually one of the permitted choices. Fine-tuning can remove some of that overhead, but then the team owns a training and maintenance process. Changing a taxonomy can become a model-development project rather than a prompt edit.

Jev offers Glean a third option: supply the decision and its candidates at inference time. That does not eliminate evaluation. Goyal describes golden datasets for the places where the product uses intelligence, an internal soak among roughly 1,500 employees, and customer A/B tests. These are deployment stages, not substitutes for one another. An offline improvement needs to survive real queries and product behavior.

The first examples are query classification and expert routing. Classification tells Glean what people are asking the product to do. Goyal reports that Jev approached the quality of a fine-tuned model without carrying the same data-cleaning, training, and hosting burden. In expert routing, he reports higher precision and about an eightfold latency reduction relative to the previous LLM-based approach. The routing change still required meaningful infrastructure work; his argument is that some changes justify that cost, not that a new endpoint makes integration disappear.

5:39:46 ↗

A suggestion has a deadline#

The most concrete example arrives at 05:39:46. Multiple generators propose follow-up actions after an answer. The ranker sees the original question, the answer, and the candidates, then chooses what to surface. This is not an open-ended writing task. The choices have already been generated.

It is also a product deadline. A suggestion that arrives several seconds after the answer can miss the moment when the user is ready to act. Goyal describes a window of roughly 1.5–2 seconds, while the old LLM ranker could take considerably longer. Here, speed changes how often a feature appears, not just an infrastructure dashboard.

Original stream, 5:42:00. The slide distinguishes ranker latency, whole-engine latency, timeouts, and invalid decisions.

The slide reports median ranker latency falling from 4.67 seconds to 0.67 seconds, and suggestion-engine timeouts from 10.2% to 0.8%. It also shows the fraction of turns surfacing a suggestion rising from 83% to 91%, with observed invalid ranker decisions falling from ten to zero. These are Glean's presented results for this system, not a guarantee for every ranking workload. The whole engine improves less dramatically than the ranker, which is exactly what one would expect when only one component changes.

Returning choice probabilities also gives the application another control surface. Different deployments can adjust thresholds rather than accepting a single universal yes/no policy. The model supplies a score; the application still decides what confidence is enough to show an action.

5:44:25 ↗

Faster judgments change the development loop#

At 05:44:25, Goyal turns to citation judging: does the cited material actually support the answer's claim? Lower-cost, faster judgments make repeated evaluation more practical. His broader claim is that improving the evaluation loop can compound into product improvements because engineers can test more changes. That is a development argument, not a measured promise of exponentially better software.

Security provides a different reason to split the work. Glean uses a recall-oriented first stage followed by a larger LLM's final assessment, with some work running alongside tool I/O. In Goyal's account, replacing the earlier classifier increased first-stage recall from 31.5% to 90.2% while reducing resource and maintenance costs. The second stage remains important: a fast screening model does not become the complete security system simply because its recall improves.

He ends with a practical boundary. If the outputs can be listed, try a decision model. If the task needs free-form composition, generation remains appropriate. Stable labels and very high volume may still justify a fine-tuned classifier; changing labels make a promptable system attractive. The point is to choose the mechanism at the level of an individual decision, then measure both its quality and its effect on the surrounding product.
