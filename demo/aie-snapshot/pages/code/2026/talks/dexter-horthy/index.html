Introduction#

A software factory already existed before coding agents. Someone identified a problem, translated it into work, built a change, ran tests, reviewed it, released it, and watched what happened. Dexter Horthy's argument is that replacing the builder does not make the rest of that system optional.

He opens with the difficulty of evolving software across successive requirements. A program that passes an initial task can become harder to change at the next checkpoint. That is a different challenge from generating a whole project when all requirements are visible at once. His discussion of code-evolution evaluation frames maintainability as behavior under future work, not just a style preference.

8:19:55 ↗

Replace one stage, preserve the loop#

At 08:19:55, Horthy reduces the transition to a single substitution: the agent builds the thing. His surrounding argument concerns everything needed before and after that step.

The pre-agent factory has sources of intent, implementation, verification, controlled rollout, and feedback from users. The agent enters that structure. It does not automatically supply product understanding, release safety, or evidence that the change helped anyone.

Horthy divides the forces acting on the factory into forward pressure and backpressure. Forward pressure creates work: feature requests, incidents, and new goals. Backpressure makes the output meet a standard: tests, linters, reviews, and other checks. An agent can iterate against those checks before consuming a human's attention.

More forward pressure without useful backpressure creates a larger pile of output. The factory needs both to improve what ships.

Spend planning effort where rework hurts#

Not every change deserves the same ceremony. Horthy proposes thinking about the likelihood and cost of rework. A low-stakes visual tweak and a database schema decision should not receive identical planning budgets.

That begins with knowing the limits of the agent and retaining enough understanding of the code to recognize a bad direction. For visual work, showing an example or obtaining a concrete preview can resolve ambiguity better than another page of instructions. For implementation, the agent needs tools that let it observe what it built: a browser, screenshots, command-line requests, or the relevant device build.

Verification must match the artifact. A successful text response is not evidence that a UI works. A passing unit test may not establish that a migration is safe. The factory needs checks that expose the failure mode the task actually risks.

Deterministic checks are especially valuable for recurring mistakes. A lint rule can enforce a convention every time, rather than relying on an instruction the agent may overlook. Past failures can become evaluations, so the system accumulates lessons instead of repeatedly paying for the same correction.

Keep experiments cheap and decisions recoverable#

Horthy leaves room for rough work. A prototype behind a feature flag can be useful precisely because it does not deserve the full cost of production hardening. The important distinction is whether the roughness undermines the intended outcome.

He also argues for keeping the context of work together. Plans, decisions, execution, and feedback become harder to use when they are scattered across unrelated chat histories and copied snippets. A factory needs a place where the next person or agent can recover why a choice was made, not merely find the final diff.

Some of this infrastructure is reusable. Some is irreducibly specific to the company. A general harness cannot know all the domain properties that make a change correct. Teams still have to build the verification and control surface that their own systems require.

Slop transfers cost to the reader#

The talk's final turn is an anti-slop argument. Producing a long document or a large pull request is becoming cheap. Understanding it is not becoming cheap at the same rate. Unreviewed output can therefore move effort from the producer to everyone who must consume it.

Horthy connects this to compounding effects. Poor material enters a knowledge base, another agent reads and imitates it, and the next result becomes harder to trust. Repeatedly working around problems rather than fixing them feeds the same cycle.

His proposed response is responsibility rather than a ban on AI assistance. Understand the problem. Own the intent and consequences. Review what you send to someone else. Make it easy for people to understand the result. Push back kindly when another person's output makes that unnecessarily difficult.

The appropriate review may be a plan, a design, a result, or a subset of code. It depends on the stakes. His point is that removing all human judgment also removes much of what would distinguish your output from anyone else's use of the same model.

A factory succeeds when it delivers a useful outcome without exhausting the people around it. Lines generated, pull requests merged, and agents running in parallel are at best intermediate measures. Forward pressure gets work started. Backpressure, feedback, and care determine whether the work deserved to ship.
