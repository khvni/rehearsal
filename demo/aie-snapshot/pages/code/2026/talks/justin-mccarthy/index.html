Brownfield factories keep the promises you already made

Justin McCarthy opens a software-factory talk by asking people to stop building software factories. The provocation has a scope: stop forcing uncertain ideas through an industrial process before you know what you want.

Introduction#

Justin McCarthy opens a software-factory talk by asking people to stop building software factories. The provocation has a scope: stop forcing uncertain ideas through an industrial process before you know what you want.

An existing product is a different case. Customers use it, pay for it, report problems, and reveal which behavior matters. That evidence gives a maintenance loop something to preserve. His distinction is between discovering the next product and continuously improving a product whose useful behavior already exists.

6:36:44 ↗

Separate discovery from preservation#

McCarthy describes early attempts to make agent loops reliable as expensive and heavily engineered. Better models and harnesses have made more of that work routine. The resulting capacity should not make every task look like a factory job.

In brownfield work, the system already has users and obligations. Runtime behavior, usage, and support feedback supply signals for checking whether a change improves or damages it. A rewrite can be valuable without requiring a fresh theory of what the customer wants.

Greenfield work lacks that settled target. If the product idea is wrong, a reliable loop can produce the wrong thing faster. McCarthy argues for different modes of work: rigor around preserving known value, experimentation around discovering new value.

6:40:25 ↗

Encode promises, not just features#

Diffusion uses “promise” for an expectation the product should continue meeting. Features are promises, but so are cost, performance, and security. Naming those expectations helps turn a vague maintenance goal into something a loop can check.

At 06:40:25, McCarthy uses a feedback circuit to explain why generating more output without feeding quality signals back into the process can amplify noise.

The analogy has a practical consequence. An agent that produces changes is only one part of the system. Tests, operational measurements, and customer-facing signals must affect what it does next. Otherwise a high activity rate can hide deterioration.

The factory should be continuous. Performance concerns and maintenance obligations do not occur only when a person opens an editor. McCarthy's desired system keeps checking known promises in the background, freeing people to investigate less settled questions.

That is a design goal, not a claim that every existing application can be safely left unattended after adding a loop. The promises still need useful checks, and changes still need the controls appropriate to the system.

6:44:58 ↗

Improve the same product without redefining it#

McCarthy expects the machinery around these loops to simplify as models improve. He compares that process to successive engine designs with fewer visible components and better performance: making the mechanism simpler can require substantial intelligence, not less work.

His suggested brownfield instruction is the same product, faster. Cheaper and safer are other directions with reasonably clear objectives, provided they do not quietly trade away another promise.

“Better” can be less well specified. A new experience, market position, or workflow may require product judgment that existing measurements cannot settle. That is where preservation ends and discovery begins again.

The distinction prevents a maintenance factory from claiming credit for deciding the business's future. It can protect and improve the value already being delivered. It cannot infer every new thing worth promising from the fact that the old thing works.

6:46:40 ↗

Production capacity creates a demand problem#

The second half asks what happens when the factory can produce more software than customers want to absorb. Customers may want higher margins, more customers of their own, or differentiation. More windows and buttons do not necessarily deliver any of those outcomes.

McCarthy calls the next exploration a “demand factory,” and explicitly presents it as an emerging idea rather than a finished architecture. Agents could help choose better market questions, synthesize answers, prioritize experiments, and respond faster to a customer opportunity.

There is a constraint on what a company can credibly offer. His sparkling-water example contrasts a plausible extension into an energy drink with an implausible extension into motor oil. Both are products someone can manufacture; only one makes sense inside the brand's existing relationship with customers.

The proposed work is therefore not simply generating more features. It is discovering adjacent promises customers will value, testing them, and matching the pace of production to the pace of learning and adoption.

The brownfield factory makes that exploration more feasible by keeping existing commitments healthy. The human task moves toward deciding which new commitments are worth making, rather than treating an ever-larger backlog of generated software as evidence of progress.
