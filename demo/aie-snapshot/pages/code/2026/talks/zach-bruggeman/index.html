How to build an agent that builds the culture

Inspect began as Ramp's collaborative coding agent.

Zach BruggemanRampPrincipal Software Engineer

Day 1 livestream · 7:48:52–8:04:03 on YouTube. The player starts and stops at this session’s boundaries.

Introduction#

Inspect began as Ramp's collaborative coding agent. Zach Bruggeman now describes it as part of the company's engineering culture: a shared place to build, investigate, review, and learn from one another's work. His talk is less concerned with how to provision a sandbox than with what changes when an agent's work is visible to a team instead of locked inside an individual's conversation.

7:49:59 ↗

A common baseline is more valuable than a thousand custom setups#

At 07:49:59, Bruggeman introduces Inspect's multiplayer sessions and integrations with Slack, Linear, telemetry, databases, and company tools. He reports that roughly 75% of Ramp's code is written and shipped through Inspect. That is his account of adoption, not a claim that three quarters of engineering judgment has disappeared.

The original technical novelty was fast sandboxes and shared context. By the time of this talk, he argues, the harder question is organizational. If every person spends fifteen minutes setting up a tool, that is already a meaningful amount of company time. More importantly, setup is not a one-time expense. Models, tools, and useful practices keep changing, and those improvements do not spread evenly through individual configurations.

Inspect gives the company a baseline that can improve centrally. A useful tool or pattern can reach everyone without each employee reconstructing it. Bruggeman says Ramp did not need to mandate adoption: the shared option was good enough that people voluntarily reached for it, while remaining free to use their own setup.

His first lesson is to remove information silos. One person cannot know the best way to use agents for every task. A common system lets an improvement discovered by one team become available to the rest, often without anyone needing to change their behavior.

7:54:07 ↗

Make working in public the easy path#

Around 07:54:07, Bruggeman explains that Inspect sessions are public within Ramp by default. Teammates can see the prompts, decomposition, and tools behind work they encounter in existing channels. They can even ask Inspect to examine recent sessions and explain how colleagues approach difficult problems.

This changes both training and product development. People learn by observing actual work rather than relying entirely on a formal “how to use AI” session. The Inspect team can also discover unexpected uses, missing tools, and recurring patterns that deserve native support. Usage becomes a source of product evidence rather than merely a count of active users.

The visibility also supports trust. A reviewer receiving a four-hundred-line diff need not infer the process from the code alone. The associated session can include the conversation, tool calls, before-and-after screenshots, and a verification agent's browser recording. These artifacts do not make a change automatically correct. They make it easier to understand what was attempted and what evidence supports it.

7:56:44 ↗

A bug report becomes a shared investigation#

Bruggeman's central story begins at 07:56:44. An engineer, Andrew, reports a problem with Inspect. Bruggeman asks Inspect to investigate, and the agent blames the newly added instrumentation. The team is skeptical: the very tracing that made the error visible appears to be causing it.

Rather than accepting that explanation, Dylan asks Inspect to inspect the telemetry library's code. The more specific diagnosis is that a fetch response body was neither read nor canceled. Bruggeman describes this as interacting with a Cloudflare platform limitation at the time. The proposed patch explicitly cancels the response body; after deployment, he says, the errors stop.

Original stream, 7:58:20. The displayed message asks Inspect to inspect the OpenTelemetry Cloudflare worker code rather than assume tracing itself is the cause.

The cultural result is more important to him than the bug fix. Previously, the report might have become a ticket, followed by private debugging and a later “fixed” message. Here, the skeptical reporter watches the investigation, contributes to it, and begins discussing other things the team could build together. The agent does not earn trust by demanding belief. Its work becomes inspectable enough for people to challenge, refine, and verify it together.

8:01:22 ↗

Let users of the tool expand its purpose#

At 08:01:22, Bruggeman gives a second example. Ramp had been rotating through off-the-shelf code-review products. Engineers noticed that Inspect already had automation triggers, including pull-request events, and used those primitives to build Review Buddy.

That work changed the Inspect team's own view of the product. They had been content to think of it as a coding agent. Other engineers pushed it toward a more complete development loop, and the team might have taken much longer to pursue that direction without the example in front of them.

The shared baseline therefore does not mean all innovation comes from the central team. It gives people a platform on which to try ideas, and a way for successful experiments to become useful to everyone. Bruggeman contrasts this with the industry's fascination with the lone operator. Individual leverage is valuable, but he does not want it to become an excuse to stop learning from other people.

He ends by asking teams to name the work they have wanted to do but postponed, then try building it together. Inspect itself began from that impulse: making colleagues' work more enjoyable. The talk's strongest claim is not that an agent creates culture by having a mascot or a personality. It is that tool defaults can make shared learning, visible evidence, and collaborative ambition easier than isolated work.
