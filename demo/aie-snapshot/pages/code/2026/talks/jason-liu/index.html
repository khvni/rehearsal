Ambition in AI

Introduction#

Jason Liu, Developer Experience at OpenAI, argues that better AI should expand what we attempt, not merely increase the number of tasks we run.

A thousand agents running in the background is an impressive quantity. It does not tell Jason Liu whether their owner is doing ambitious work. He wants to know what the person is trying to make, whether they care about the result, and whether they can recognize when it needs another pass.

That shifts the conversation away from token consumption. Liu opens with three fears he hears about AI: more low-quality work, weaker critical thinking, and eventual replacement. His response is a set of responsibilities for the person directing the tools: own the work, develop the language to judge it, and stay curious enough to enter unfamiliar fields.

0:22:20 ↗

Own the thing you ship#

At 00:22:20, Liu reaches for the camera rather than the compiler. A machine that could reproduce an image threatened one kind of painting skill. It also made photography and film possible, and eventually put cameras in the hands of people who could not previously afford one.

He makes that expansion personal. His family did not have many photographs of his childhood. He would have welcomed the ability to capture those moments, even if universal access also meant far more forgettable pictures in everyone's camera roll. Counting mediocre outputs misses the value of the important ones that otherwise would not exist.

The useful distinction is between identifying as a painter and identifying as an artist. The artist can change mediums without surrendering the intention behind the work. Liu extends the analogy to engineering: a table belongs to its maker, not the power-tool brand; a director can own the creative result of a large crew; open-source maintainers remain responsible for quality when many contributors participate.

AI makes it tempting to step away from that responsibility. The output becomes something the model did, and defects become someone else's fault. Liu calls attention to the quieter failure: becoming a spectator of your own work. The practical countermeasure is ordinary product care. Use the application, notice the broken behavior, insist on another iteration. An impressive result shared online may represent many rounds of that attention, not a single miraculous prompt.

0:27:02 ↗

Taste needs a vocabulary#

Ownership creates a harder question: how do you know when the result is good enough?

Liu's art-school experience was full of work he could dislike but could not yet improve. His judgment exceeded his ability to execute. Telling himself to make the work more exciting did not close the gap. He needed names for what he was seeing: space, value, shape, texture, and the relationships between them.

The same problem appears when an engineer asks an agent to improve an interface. A complaint without a model of the defect gives the agent little to work with. Learning typography can turn a vague dislike into a specific decision about hierarchy or emphasis. Studying motion and interaction can reveal why an application feels awkward even when each screen looks acceptable.

The slide at 29:00 gives a concrete example of useful vocabulary: identifying implicit state machines creates a more specific engineering question than asking an agent to improve an app.

Liu uses a generator-and-critic analogy to describe the next constraint. If the model can already produce everything its human critic knows how to ask for, more generation alone cannot tell the critic what should change. The human needs to improve their ability to discriminate between alternatives. In this framing, stronger models make learning more valuable: execution can catch up with judgment quickly, so judgment has to keep moving.

He gives the word taste a practical meaning: the ability to describe what you like and anticipate what others will like. It is not an inherited quality that excuses an unexplained preference. It is a working model that can be expanded through exposure, comparison, and practice.

0:30:41 ↗

Give yourself something better to ask for#

At 00:30:41, Liu turns that idea into a way of spending time. A person making games should play games and notice the animation, pacing, and stories that work. A person building tools should use other tools closely enough to understand their choices. A person making advertisements should study advertisements.

His own 3D-printing experiments initially produced shapes that looked like rectangles glued together. Repeatedly asking for something more beautiful was frustrating. Looking at industrial design, sports cars, and physical objects gave him a richer language for curves and proportions. The improvement came from having a clearer idea of what he wanted, not from finding a stronger synonym for beautiful.

This also changes what a productive team should permit. If AI saves time, Liu argues, some of that time should become room to explore other products and experiences. A team that converts every saved minute into more output may leave its judgment unchanged while increasing its ability to reproduce the same limitations.

0:32:46 ↗

Return to the interests you put aside#

The final part of the talk, beginning around 00:32:46, asks engineers what they abandoned in order to pursue software. Music? Physical design? A scientific interest whose prerequisites once seemed too demanding?

Liu is not proposing that a tool instantly supplies expertise in all those fields. His claim is that it lowers the cost of entering them. Experienced engineers already bring habits of experimentation, decomposition, and debugging. AI can help them learn the unfamiliar software and language needed to apply those habits elsewhere.

He describes learning drums, connecting instruments through MIDI, building custom music software, exploring Ableton, and designing physical parts. The experience matters partly because it makes him a beginner again. A beginner may ask a question that an established practitioner has stopped considering; the engineer's experience helps turn that question into an experiment.

The talk ends with a responsibility to the next generation. Learning to code was often motivated by making something useful rather than completing a textbook first. With the range of possible projects expanding, engineers can demonstrate a wider set of things worth attempting. Liu's version of ambition is to make work that you care about, learn enough to improve it, and show other people what becomes possible when they do the same.
