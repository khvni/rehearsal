Build the company around the agent, not a pilot project

Introduction#

Quinn Slack's obstacle was not getting permission to use an agent. He was the CEO. The difficulty was changing a company whose existing customers, roles, and operating habits had been built around something else.

He begins with his early experience of a coding agent and the attempt to build Amp inside Sourcegraph. A small team could move quickly, but it still lived inside the expectations of a much larger enterprise company. His account of spinning Amp out is therefore an organizational argument: if agents change the cost and speed of work, the surrounding organization may need to change too.

Constraints can force a different operating model#

Slack describes a small team unable, for a period, to hire as it normally would while the separation was being worked through. That limitation made broader ownership a necessity. People had to solve problems outside a narrow functional lane rather than route every need toward another department.

He is not presenting a controlled comparison between companies. This is an operator's explanation of the choices behind Amp. His central claim is that an agent product is difficult to differentiate through a static feature list alone. Features are copied quickly. A company must learn from users and ship improvements at a pace its structure can sustain.

The product and the organization reinforce each other. Agent threads hold useful context for work, support, and collaboration. Bringing another person into a thread can be more effective than reconstructing the problem in a separate communication channel. The work itself becomes something colleagues can inspect and continue.

Small changes make production informative#

Slack's most provocative slide advocates pushing to main without conventional code review. Its supporting argument is not that verification no longer matters. It is that large batches, long waits, and detached approval can create their own risk.

At 08:04:05, Slack reports roughly 130 deployments a day and about ten minutes from push to production. These are Amp's presented operating figures, not a recommendation to remove review from every system.

His example starts by adding logging to test whether the engineer understands a problem. A later change can run old and new behavior side by side. Once that comparison is understood, another small deployment can change the live path. The deployment is a step in learning, rather than a single final ceremony after a large implementation.

This makes observability and ownership central. Someone must notice what the change does and decide the next increment. Simply deleting a review requirement would not reproduce the feedback loop he describes.

Agents also expand what can be checked. Slack describes using them to examine interface behavior, including frame-by-frame visual issues, and to explore multiple design variations. The point is not to stop judging a result because a model produced it. It is to spend cheap generation and inspection on getting a better result.

Finish the support loop#

A customer report can become a fix, but Slack pushes the sequence further. After fixing the issue, the team can apologize, issue credits when appropriate, search for other affected users, and contact them before they complain. That creates another source of feedback about the product.

The important move is connecting activities that often live in separate queues. Support learns what happened. Engineering changes the behavior. Logs identify the wider impact. Customer communication closes the loop. Agents make more of that sequence feasible for the person who owns the problem.

He extends the same idea to internal tools. A company may not need to reproduce an entire large SaaS product. It may need a small, well-integrated slice that fits its own work. An employee outside a conventional engineering role can build a useful payment or operational workflow rather than wait for a dedicated project team.

Broad ownership still needs a direction#

Slack describes a company with few meetings and people who spend time with customers rather than depending on a separate product-management layer to translate all demand. Dictation and agent tools reduce friction in expressing intent and implementing it. The organization tries to preserve the energy of the person closest to a problem.

That model has tradeoffs. The talk does not establish that every company should remove specialist roles, adopt Amp's deployment policy, or rebuild its software stack. Its examples depend on a small team, an agent-native product, and people willing to own consequences across boundaries.

The useful question is narrower: which organizational delays still exist because producing and checking work used to be expensive? Slack's answer is to revisit those delays together, rather than insert a coding agent into an unchanged approval chain. Faster implementation matters most when the company can turn it into faster understanding, a better product, and a completed response to the customer.
