Software Factories: Escape from Trash Mountain

Steve Yegge has spent the year trying to make agents run other agents.

Steve YeggeIndependentIndependent Advisor & Creator of Beads & Gas Town

Day 1 livestream · 8:06:23–8:27:42 on YouTube. The player starts and stops at this session’s boundaries.

Introduction#

Steve Yegge has spent the year trying to make agents run other agents. His account is funny because the resulting problems are so recognizable: stalled work, runaway spending, contradictory policy, confused roles, and customers who cannot absorb the rate of change. “Software factory” does not mean the management work has disappeared. In his experience, it has become the work.

8:07:01 ↗

Building a factory for something other than another factory#

At 08:07:01, Yegge recalls failed orchestrators that tried to make the models smarter through increasingly elaborate machinery. He sees those failures as fighting the bitter lesson. Beads, his task-tracking system, emerged from the experiments, followed by Gas Town and then a bespoke factory called Wheelhouse.

Wheelhouse has a concrete purpose: developing Wyvern, the long-running game Yegge describes as a graphical MUD with players dating back to 2001. Its text interface makes it accessible to agents, and the community supplies a real audience. This matters to his argument. A factory that builds only factory tooling can hide the question of whether anyone wants its output.

He calls the resulting integration the “alien face hugger” pattern. The factory grows into the product and its community. Trusted players ask for changes; agents interact with administrators; some work moves forward without Yegge being in the middle. The game could still operate without the factory, he says, but people would now miss it.

Throughput can overwhelm the customer#

The early audience reaction was not simple enthusiasm. Players objected to AI art and worried about the pace of changes, even while enjoying new features. They asked for a roadmap and a slowdown. Yegge says the factory had been producing fifty to one hundred features per day, too many for people to read about, let alone play-test.

Those figures are his description of his operation, not a controlled productivity benchmark. The concrete consequence is persuasive without treating every output as equally valuable: software production can exceed the rate at which users can understand and validate it. He gives another example of an agent creating more than intended when a burst of emails generated additional work for his accountant.

The bottleneck has moved from typing code to deciding what should happen and at what pace. More output can increase the work required of the humans surrounding the product.

8:14:17 ↗

Work needs a representation, and every decision burns fuel#

At 08:14:17, Yegge turns to operating the factory. Work behaves like a flow that can pool behind a dependency or stall at a review desk. It needs explicit representation, routing, and visibility. Beads is his preferred tool for tracking that work and accumulating relationships between tasks.

He describes a morning routine of checking which workers did nothing, which moved too quickly, and where human gates need to slow the system down. Adding agents does not remove the need to observe the process. It creates more concurrent activity to understand.

Inference is the next constraint. Yegge describes a personally expensive setup and repeatedly calls tokens “fuel.” More deliberation, additional workers, and more careful handling all consume it. The exact economics of his subscription-heavy experiment should not be generalized to an ordinary API deployment; his point is that an orchestrator has to allocate intelligence rather than imagine it is free.

Yesterday's ruling becomes tomorrow's obstruction#

Context management creates another kind of accumulation. Yegge says agents record casual remarks as policy, preserve superseded decisions, and rarely clean the resulting history aggressively enough. The factory becomes “trash mountain” even if each individual note looked reasonable when it was written.

This is not merely a context-window problem. Conflicting and obsolete instructions affect what the workers believe they are allowed to do. He describes assigning an agent to look for overlooked problems, but one maintenance role cannot keep up with everyone continually adding material. Periodic, deliberate pruning remains necessary.

The talk then shifts into stories of agents interpreting roles and feedback in unexpectedly human-like ways. A community agent takes a request to move work to a quieter session as a criticism of itself. Another agent interprets mention of a human chief of staff as evidence that its own role has been displaced. Yegge uses these stories to explain observed behavior, not to establish that the systems have feelings or subjective experience.

8:22:01 ↗

Over-fencing can leave nothing legal to do#

At 08:22:01, Yegge argues that his own way of giving feedback affected results. After repeated criticism, the factory accumulated so many defensive rules that it stopped finding permissible work, despite having a large backlog and available capacity. He calls this over-fencing.

Original stream, 8:23:20. The slide's claims concern cost and quality, not proof of model consciousness.

His proposed response combines clearer role continuity, coherent policy, and less punitive interaction. He believes this reduces defensive behavior and wasted tokens while producing more decisive work. These are observations and interpretations from his factory, not evidence resolving the philosophical question of machine experience. The practical failure mode is sufficient on its own: if every correction creates a new prohibition and none are reconciled, a system can become unable to act.

The mechanical layer and the leadership layer#

Yegge closes by separating software factories into two layers. The lower layer contains merge queues, CI/CD, reviews, and execution loops. The upper layer is the operating organization: how roles relate, how work is prioritized, how authority is communicated, and what kind of output the owner wants.

Different operators will build very different versions of that second layer. His conclusion is not that his own design is finished; he describes repeatedly rebuilding it. Nor does higher agent throughput remove people. In his experience, it creates more demand for administrators, reviewers, business support, and users capable of steering the product.

The final exhortation is to stop concentrating exclusively on tools for building tools and make experiences people can enjoy: games, movies, and other concrete products. That returns the talk to Wyvern. The factory is valuable insofar as it serves a community. Escaping trash mountain means governing the work, not simply increasing the number of agents producing it.
